Est. 1826

Two centuries at the same oyster bar

Union Oyster House is America's oldest restaurant in continuous service, housed in a building that was already old when it opened. The oyster bar took its place at the front door in 1826 and has not moved an inch since — through fire, war, and two hundred years of Boston appetites.

The Ledger

What the building has seen

Long before it was a restaurant, 41 Union Street was already keeping Boston's history — a dry-goods shop, a printing press, even a king in exile. Scroll the ledger below to see how a building becomes a landmark.

1704

Hopestill Capen House

The brick building rises as a dry-goods shop selling silks and fabric — decades before it ever serves a meal.

1771

A Press on the Second Floor

Isaiah Thomas prints the Massachusetts Spy upstairs, needling the Crown a few years before Lexington and Concord.

1796

A King in Exile

Louis Philippe, future King of France, lodges above the building and is said to have given French lessons to Boston's daughters to pay his way.

1826

The Oyster Bar Opens

Atwood & Bacon open the Union Oyster House. The semicircular mahogany bar takes its place at the front door — and never moves again.

1850s

Senator Webster's Stool

Daniel Webster becomes a regular, famously working through plates of oysters with a tall tumbler of brandy at his elbow.

1960s

Booth 18

A young senator named John F. Kennedy claims a corner booth upstairs for the Sunday papers and a bowl of lobster stew.

Today

Still Pouring

A National Historic Landmark, and still the oldest restaurant operating continuously in the United States.
